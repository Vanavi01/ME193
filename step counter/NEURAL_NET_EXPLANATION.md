# How the step-counting neural network works

The network reads the ankle sensor (three acceleration channels and three gyroscope channels, 100 samples per second, each channel rescaled to zero mean and unit spread) and produces, for every 10 ms of the recording, a *step rate* in steps per second. Adding up that rate over any stretch of time gives the number of steps taken in it, so the count is simply the area under the output curve. It is a 1-D convolutional network written in PyTorch: four layers, each sliding 32 small learned filters (9 samples wide) along the time axis, followed by a ReLU nonlinearity, and then a final 1x1 layer and a softplus that keeps the rate from going negative. The filters are spaced out by 1, 2, 4 and 8 samples ("dilation"), so each output sample can look about 0.6 s into the past and the future, enough to see one whole leg swing. The whole network has 29,537 weights. To teach it, every step found in the recordings (from the peaks of the leg-swing gyroscope signal, checked against hand counts) is turned into a small bump of area one on a target rate curve.

Training is a loop of four moves, repeated 1000 times on random 5-second crops of the recordings (with a little added noise and gain change so it cannot memorize exact values). First the forward pass pushes the crop through the layers and gets a predicted rate curve. Second, the loss measures how wrong it is: the mean squared difference from the target curve, plus a penalty on the error in the total count. Third, back-propagation (`loss.backward()` in PyTorch) applies the chain rule from the loss backward through the layers, giving for every one of the 29,537 weights the answer to "if this weight were slightly larger, would the loss go up or down, and by how much?". Fourth, the Adam optimizer nudges each weight a small step in the direction that lowers the loss, with a step size adapted per weight. After many repetitions the filters settle into detectors for the shape of a leg swing. We train five networks from different random starting weights; the spread between them measures how much the answer depends on luck.

It works only partly. We had five recordings (about 5 minutes and 308 steps), so we tested by leaving one recording out, training on the other four, and counting the one it had never seen, for each recording in turn. The ankle-only network's count was off by 13.9% on average (95% confidence interval 8% to 23%; steps were counted from the labels or by hand), worst on the stop-and-go recording with two long pauses (39 steps counted against 57). A plain peak detector, which also produced the training labels, was off by 2.2%, and the phone's built-in pedometer by 4.1%, so the network did not beat the simple methods. Adding the phone sensors made it worse (50% error with every channel), most likely because with so little data the network learned to recognize each recording by the angle the phone sat at in the pocket instead of learning what a step looks like. More recordings would be the first thing to try.
